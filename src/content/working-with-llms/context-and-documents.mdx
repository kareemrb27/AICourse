A model only knows what it learned in training plus what is in the conversation right now. Most of the difference between a useless answer and an excellent one is what you put in front of it. This lesson covers how to supply that material well.

## The context window, in practice

Everything the model can use at one time sits in its **context window**: your instructions, earlier messages, attached files, search results, and the answer it is writing. The window is measured in tokens and has a limit.

Four consequences:

1. **The model does not know your files, systems or history** unless you provide them.
2. **Long chats fill the window.** Old details can be dropped, summarized or ignored, so long threads drift.
3. **More is not always better.** Irrelevant material can distract the model and raise cost.
4. **Position matters.** Research on long inputs has found that models can use information at the start and end of a long text better than information buried in the middle.

You can see how text becomes tokens with a free tokenizer tool, which makes limits and costs feel concrete.

## Give it the source, not a description

Compare:

- "Summarize our refund policy." The model will invent a plausible generic policy.
- "Summarize the refund policy below for a new support agent." followed by the policy text. The model can now be accurate.

Rule of thumb: **if the answer must be based on a specific document, include that document**, and tell the model to use only it.

```text
Answer using only the document below. If the answer is not in the document,
say "not stated" and do not guess. Quote the sentence you relied on.

<document>
...policy text...
</document>

Question: Can a customer get a refund after 45 days?
```

Asking for the quote makes the answer much easier to check.

## Working with long documents

- **Put the document first and the question after it**, and label both clearly.
- **Ask narrow questions** rather than "tell me everything".
- **Ask for evidence**: quotes, section names or page numbers.
- **Use two passes for big material**: first extract the relevant parts, then reason over them.
- **Split by section** when a document is much longer than the window, then combine the partial summaries.
- **Check the extraction.** Uploaded files can be read imperfectly: tables, scanned pages and images are common trouble spots.

## Common document tasks

| Task | A good instruction |
| --- | --- |
| Summarize | "Five bullets for a manager who has two minutes. Put the decision needed first." |
| Extract | "List every date, owner and deadline as a table. Use 'unknown' if missing." |
| Compare | "Compare version A and B. List changes that affect security or cost." |
| Review | "Find contradictions or missing information. Quote the lines involved." |
| Answer | "Answer only from the text. Quote your source. Say 'not stated' if unsure." |

## Keep conversations healthy

- **Start a new chat for a new task.** Old context can leak into new answers.
- **Restate key facts** when a thread gets long, or ask for a summary and paste it into a fresh chat.
- **Use projects or custom instructions** for standing context such as your role, house style and recurring documents, so you do not repeat yourself.
- **Be careful with memory features.** They are convenient, and they also store information about you. Know what is saved and how to turn it off.

## Context for you, not only for the model

Good context also helps *you* check the answer. If the assistant quotes the source text, you can look it up. If it works from memory, you cannot tell where a claim came from.
