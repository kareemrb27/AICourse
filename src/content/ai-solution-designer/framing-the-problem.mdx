You have now learned how AI works, how to use it, how to build with it and how to test it. Now comes the skill that makes people valuable: deciding **what is worth building**, and **how** to turn a vague wish ("we should use AI for support") into a problem someone could actually solve, measure and ship. Most failed AI projects fail here, long before any model is chosen: they solve a problem nobody had, or one that a simpler tool would have solved, or one whose success nobody defined. This lesson teaches you to frame problems the way a solution designer does.

## Start with the problem, not the technology

"We need an AI strategy" and "let's add a chatbot" are solutions looking for a problem. A solution designer reverses the order:

1. **Who** has the problem, and what are they trying to get done?
2. **What** gets in their way today, and what does it cost, in time, money, errors or frustration?
3. **What** would better look like, in a number you can measure?
4. **Only then**: is AI the right tool, and which kind?

Try it on a real example. Compare these two descriptions of the same work:

| Technology-first | Problem-first |
| --- | --- |
| "Add an AI assistant to the service desk." | "Service desk agents spend about 9 minutes per ticket finding the right policy, and 15 percent of first replies are wrong, which causes reopened tickets. Cut time-to-first-reply by a third without raising the wrong-reply rate." |

The second can be built, measured and judged. The first cannot.

## The problem statement

Write every candidate in one short structure, and do not move on until you can fill it in:

```text
PROBLEM STATEMENT

Who:            <the people who have the problem, and how many>
Job to be done: <what they are trying to achieve>
Today:          <how it is done now, how long it takes, how often it goes wrong>
Pain:           <what it costs: hours, money, errors, risk, customer impact>
Better means:   <a measurable target, such as "first reply in 6 minutes, wrong replies under 10%">
Constraints:    <data we may use, systems it must fit, rules, deadlines, budget>
Stakes:         <what happens if the solution is wrong, and who is affected>
Not in scope:   <what we are deliberately not trying to do>
```

The most overlooked line is **Today**. You cannot say whether a solution is better until you know how the work is done now, so **measure the baseline first**: time a sample of real cases, count errors, and talk to the people doing it. Many AI projects discover that the real problem is different from the one that was described.

## Find the right problems

Good candidates for AI often share these traits:

- **Language or pattern work**: reading, summarizing, drafting, classifying, extracting, searching.
- **High volume** of similar tasks, so small savings add up.
- **Tolerable errors**, or errors that are easy to catch, because the model is sometimes wrong.
- **Available data or documents** that contain what the system needs.
- **People who want it**, who will use it and give feedback.

Weak candidates: tasks where one error is catastrophic and cannot be caught, where the answer is a simple lookup or rule, where no data exists, where nobody owns the process, or where the real obstacle is organizational, not informational.

A useful early question: **"What would a clever person with a spreadsheet and an hour do?"** If a rule, a form, a better search box or a process change solves it, that may beat AI on cost, speed and risk.

## Score the ideas

Collect ten candidate ideas from the people who do the work, and rank them on the same criteria. Rate each from 1 to 5. This code applies one transparent formula. Change the weights to fit your organization and the ranking changes with them, which is a useful conversation in itself:

```python
import pandas as pd

IDEAS = [
    {"idea": "Draft first replies to support tickets",     "value": 5, "feasibility": 4, "data": 4, "risk": 2, "effort": 3},
    {"idea": "Search our policies in plain language",       "value": 4, "feasibility": 5, "data": 5, "risk": 2, "effort": 2},
    {"idea": "Auto-approve expense claims",                 "value": 4, "feasibility": 3, "data": 3, "risk": 5, "effort": 4},
    {"idea": "Summarize incident reports for management",  "value": 3, "feasibility": 5, "data": 4, "risk": 1, "effort": 1},
    {"idea": "Predict which servers will fail",             "value": 4, "feasibility": 2, "data": 2, "risk": 3, "effort": 4},
    {"idea": "Screen job applicants",                       "value": 3, "feasibility": 3, "data": 3, "risk": 5, "effort": 4},
]

# Benefits count for more than costs. These weights are a starting point, not a law.
WEIGHTS = {"value": 3, "feasibility": 2, "data": 2, "risk": -3, "effort": -1}


def score_ideas(ideas, weights=WEIGHTS):
    table = pd.DataFrame(ideas)
    table["score"] = sum(table[column] * weight for column, weight in weights.items())
    table["red_flags"] = table.apply(red_flags, axis=1)
    return table.sort_values("score", ascending=False)


def red_flags(row):
    flags = []
    if row["data"] <= 2:
        flags.append("data is weak")
    if row["feasibility"] <= 2:
        flags.append("hard to build")
    if row["risk"] >= 5:
        flags.append("high stakes: needs human decisions and a full review")
    return "; ".join(flags)


print(score_ideas(IDEAS)[["idea", "score", "red_flags"]].to_string(index=False))
```

Treat the output as a **conversation starter**, not an answer. Look at what rises and falls, check whether the ranking matches your judgment, and when it does not, find out why. Pay special attention to the **red flags**: a high-scoring idea that touches people's rights, money or safety needs the responsible-AI review from earlier in this path, and sometimes the right decision is to leave it out, or to keep a human firmly in charge.

## Define success before you build

Decide what "good" means **in numbers**, and which numbers must not get worse:

| Kind of measure | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Outcome** (the point) | Time to resolve, cases handled per person, cost per case, customer satisfaction |
| **Quality** (guardrails) | Accuracy on a test set, wrong-reply rate, complaints, escalations |
| **Adoption** | Share of eligible people using it, how often, whether they keep using it |
| **Risk** | Incidents, policy breaches, harmful outputs, data exposures |
| **Cost** | Cost per task, total monthly spend, effort to maintain |

Pick **one or two outcome measures**, a few guardrails, and a **threshold** for each. Write them down before the pilot, as you did with the launch gate. People will be tempted to redefine success after seeing the results.

## Understand the people

Technology succeeds or fails through people. Before designing, talk to:

- **The users**: watch them work. What do they do, what do they hate, what will they not give up?
- **The owners of the process**: who is accountable, and what constraints do they live with?
- **The people affected** but not using it, such as customers or colleagues downstream.
- **The people who can stop it**: security, privacy, legal, compliance and works councils.
- **The sponsor**: who pays, and what do they need to see to continue?

Map their interests early. A solution that delights the users but alarms the security team will not ship.

## Ask the uncomfortable questions

- What happens when it is **wrong**? Who finds out, and how fast?
- **Who loses** if this works? Jobs, status and habits change, and people notice.
- Is the data **ours to use** for this purpose?
- Would we be comfortable if a **customer or regulator** saw exactly how this works?
- What is the **simplest version** that would still teach us something?
- What would make us **stop**?

## Choose one idea

From your ranked list, pick the one with the best mix of value, feasibility and learning, and a sponsor who cares. A good first project is **valuable but not critical**, uses data you already have, has a clear owner and can show a result in weeks, not years. Write the problem statement, measure the baseline and move on to choosing an approach.
