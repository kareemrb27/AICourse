Good prompts are half the job. The other half is the responses, and for preference tuning, the comparisons between them. This lesson covers how responses are produced and judged.

## Producing responses

### Distillation from a stronger model
Ask a stronger "teacher" model to answer, and train a smaller "student" on the results. Orca showed the value of asking the teacher for **explanation traces** (step-by-step reasoning, system prompts that request careful thought) rather than just final answers, so the student learns how to reason as well as what to say.

### Self-generated answers with checking
Where answers can be **verified**, sample several responses and keep only those that pass: code that runs against tests, math with a known result, structured output that validates against a schema. This is called rejection sampling, and it yields cleaner data than trusting any single generation.

### Human-in-the-loop
Review a sample. People catch problems automatic filters miss, and a few hundred human-corrected examples can anchor a larger synthetic set.

## Preference data

Preference tuning methods such as DPO need pairs: for a prompt, a **chosen** response and a **rejected** one. Ways to create them:

- **Multiple samples, then rank.** Generate several responses (ideally from different models or temperatures) and score them.
- **Judge models.** An LLM grades each response against a rubric. UltraFeedback built a large preference dataset with AI-provided ratings and critiques.
- **Reward models.** A trained scorer ranks responses. Nemotron-4's pipeline used a reward model rating attributes like helpfulness, correctness, coherence, complexity and verbosity.
- **Principles-based feedback.** Constitutional AI replaces human harmlessness labels with AI feedback guided by written principles.

## Judges need validation

LLM judges are useful but biased. Studies of LLM-as-a-judge document issues such as favoring longer answers and position effects when comparing two responses. Protect yourself:

1. Write an explicit **rubric** with examples of each score.
2. **Randomize order** when comparing pairs, or score twice with positions swapped.
3. **Hand-label a sample**, measure agreement, and revise until acceptable.
4. Use a **different model** to judge than the one that generated, where possible.
5. Prefer **verifiable** checks over judgment when you can.

## Make the margin meaningful

For DPO-style pairs, a chosen answer that is only slightly better than the rejected one teaches little. Keep pairs with a clear gap, and check that chosen answers are not just longer.

<Callout title="Common trap">
  If your chosen responses are systematically longer, the tuned model learns
  to be verbose. Track length distributions for chosen versus rejected, and
  balance them.
</Callout>

## Check yourself

1. Take 50 prompts. Generate 4 responses each, and judge them with a rubric.
2. Hand-label 20 of the judgments. Compute agreement and refine the rubric.
3. Build chosen and rejected pairs. Compare length and quality of chosen versus rejected.
